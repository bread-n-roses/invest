  1. Question

    Consider a risky asset that offers an expected return of 6.1% and a standard deviation of 13.3%.
    T-bills offer a risk-free rate of 3.1%.

    Suppose Laura is a mean-variance optimizer with utility function:

    U=𝔼(rp)−12Aσp2U = \mathbb{E}(r_p) - \frac{1}{2} A \sigma_p^2

    Because of her risk preferences and the restriction that short-selling is not possible, she chooses to invest all of her money in the risky asset (w*=1w^{*} = 1).

    In this context, what is the maximum level of risk aversion AA that would justify Laura’s choice?

    Round your answer to two decimal places. For example: 3.58.


    Solution

    We know that the optimal weight in the risky asset is: w*=𝔼(r)−rfA⋅σ2w^* = \frac{\mathbb{E}(r) - r_f}{A \cdot \sigma^2}.

    In this case Laura chooses w*=1w^{*} = 1 because short-selling is not allowed, suggesting that she might also prefer w*≥1w^{*} \geq 1. As w*>1w^{*} > 1 is restricted, she chooses w*=1w^{*} = 1 instead. In any case her preference is not w*<1w^{*} < 1, because she did not choose so.

    Solving for AA under the constraint w*≥1w^* \geq 1, we find: 𝔼(r)−rfA⋅σ2≥1\frac{\mathbb{E}(r) - r_f}{A \cdot \sigma^2} \geq 1, which becomes 𝔼(r)−rfσ2≥A\frac{\mathbb{E}(r) - r_f}{\sigma^2} \geq A.

    Filling in what we know: A≤𝔼(r)−rfσ2=0.061−0.0310.1332=1.7A \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}(r) - r_f}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.061 - 0.031}{0.133^2} = 1.7. Hence, AA is at most 1.7.


  2. Question

    Suppose that you own a portfolio that yields the following weekly returns over a month:

    • Week 1: 3.6%
    • Week 2: -1.7%
    • Week 3: -0.9%
    • Week 4: 1.7%

    What is the return of your portfolio after one month using weekly compounding?

    Round your answer to four decimal places. For example, 1.4872% or 0.014872 becomes 0.0149.


    Solution

    (1+3.6/100)⋅(1−1.7/100)⋅(1−0.9/100)⋅(1+1.7/100)−1=0.0264(1 + 3.6/100) \cdot (1 - 1.7/100) \cdot (1 - 0.9/100) \cdot (1 + 1.7/100) - 1 = 0.0264


  3. Question

    You are comparing two portfolios, A and B. Consider Roy’s Safety-First Ratio (SFR).

    • Portfolio A has an SFR of 1.07, based on monthly observations.
    • Portfolio B has an SFR of 3.39, based on yearly observations.

    What is the SFR, in yearly terms, of the worst-performing portfolio?

    Report your answer with two decimals. For example, 0.62.


    Solution

    To compare fairly, we need to annualize Portfolio A’s SFR:
    SFRA,yearly=SFRA,monthly⋅12=1.07⋅12=3.71\text{SFR}_{A, \text{yearly}} = \text{SFR}_{A, \text{monthly}} \cdot \sqrt{12} = 1.07 \cdot \sqrt{12} = 3.71

    Then compare this to Portfolio B’s given SFR: 3.39.

    The worst-performing portfolio is the one with the lower SFR, which is: min⁡(3.71,3.39)=3.39\min(3.71,\ 3.39) = 3.39.

  12. Question

    Consider the following information about the excess returns of two securities and the statements below:

    Expectation (%) Standard deviation (%) Risk-free rate (%) Minimum required return (%) Skewness Loss probability
    Security A 6 30 0 2 -0.4 0.2
    Security B 3 15 0 2 +0.4 0.8

    Statement (i): The Sharpe ratios for both securities are equal.

    Statement (ii): The Safety-First ratios for both securities are equal.


    1. Only Statement (i) is correct
    2. Only Statement (ii) is correct
    3. Both statements are correct
    4. None of the statements is correct

    Solution

    1. True. The Sharpe ratios are 6/30 and 3/15, which is 0.2 for both securities. Minimum required return (MRP), skewness, and loss probabilities are not considered for the Sharpe ratio. The Safety first ratio (SFR) considers the total return, net of the MRP, divided by the standard deviation of total returns. As the risk-free rate is zero, excess returns and total returns are equal, and so is the standard deviation for excess and for total returns. The SFR ratios are then: Security A: (6-2) / 30 = 0.1333 Security B: (3-2) / 15 = 0.0666.
    2. False.
    3. False.
    4. False
