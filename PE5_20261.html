  1. Question

    You purchased one American call option for $2.99\$2.99 which will expire tomorrow. The call has a strike price of $48\$48, and the stock is now trading for $52.8\$52.8.

    If you exercise the call today, what will be your holding-period return?

    Report your answer as a fraction and round your answer to two decimal places, for example: 0.54.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 0.610.61.

    If the call is exercised the gross profit is $52.8−$48=$4.8\$52.8 − \$48 = \$4.8, and the net profit is $4.8−$2.99=$1.81\$4.8 − \$2.99 = \$1.81. The holding period return is then $1.81/$2.99=0.61\$1.81 / \$2.99 = 0.61 (61%).


  2. Question

    A put option with a strike price of $50\$50 is currently selling for $5.1. The hedge ratio for the put is -0.5, and the stock is currently worth $54\$54.

    What is the elasticity of the put? Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, -4.71.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: −5.29-5.29.

    The elasticity of a put option is given by ΔPΔS\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta S}. We consider the case that the stock price increases by $1.

    The current stock price is $54\$54, the put premium is $5.1\$5.1, and the hedge ratio is −0.5-0.5.

    The change in the put premium is ΔP=($5.1+$1⋅−0.5−$5.1)/$5.1≈−0.098\Delta P = (\$5.1 + \$1\cdot -0.5 - \$5.1)/\$5.1 \approx -0.098

    The change in the stock price is ΔS=($55−$54)/$54≈0.0185\Delta S = (\$55 - \$54)/\$54 \approx 0.0185.

    The option elasticity is then −0.098/0.0185≈−5.29-0.098/0.0185 \approx -5.29.


  3. Question

    Consider a two-state binomial tree. Suppose you are attempting to value a 66-month expiration option on a stock with volatility (i.e., annualized standard deviation) of σ=0.34\sigma = 0.34.

    Given that your binomial model is structured with 10 subperiods, what should be the appropriate value for the down factor dd?

    Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, 0.89.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 0.930.93.

    The down-factor can be calculated as exp⁡(−σΔt)\exp(-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}) where Δt=T/n\Delta t = T/n, with TT being the total time to expiration and nn being the number of subperiods.

    In this case, we have 10 subperiods in 6 months. Our Δt=(1/12)⋅6/10=0.05\Delta t = (1/12) \cdot 6/10 = 0.05. Multiplying with (1/12)(1/12) is to ensure we are dealing with Δt\Delta t that is expressed in years, as the volatility is annualized.

    The volatility is 0.34. The down factor then becomes exp⁡(−0.340.05)≈0.93\exp(-0.34 \sqrt{0.05}) \approx 0.93.


  4. Question

    Suppose the current price on a non-dividend paying stock is $47\$47. The annualized risk-free rate in the market is 0.05. Consider a call option on this stock with a strike price of $39\$39 and a time to expiration of half a year.

    Use the Black-Scholes formula to find the price of the call option.

    Use N(d1)=0.9414N(d_1) = 0.9414 and N(d2)=0.923N(d_2) = 0.923 to simplify your calculations.

    Round your answer to two decimals. For example, 11.63.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 9.149.14.

    The Black-Scholes call price is given by C0=S0N(d1)−Xexp⁡(−rf⋅T)N(d2)C_0 = S_0 N(d_1) - X\exp(-rf \cdot T) N(d_2).

    The current stock price is $47\$47, the risk-free rate is 0.05 and the strike price is $39\$39, so the call option price is: 47⋅0.9414−39⋅exp⁡(−0.05⋅0.5)⋅0.923≈$9.1375691≈$9.1447 \cdot 0.9414 - 39 \cdot \exp(-0.05 \cdot 0.5) \cdot 0.923 \approx \$9.1375691 \approx \$9.14.


  5. Question

    Consider a 1-year option with strike price $47\$47, on a stock with annual standard deviation 0.180.18. The T-bill rate is 3% per year.

    What is d2d_2 in the Black-Scholes world when the current stock price is $44\$44?

    Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, -0.52.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: −0.29-0.29.

    Recall that d1=ln(S0/X)+(r+σ2/2)TσTd_1 = \frac{ln(S_0/X) + (r + \sigma^2/2) T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.

    Filling in what we know, we obtain d1=ln(44/47)+(0.03+(0.182)/2)⋅10.181≈−0.1097665d_1 = \frac{ln(44/47) + (0.03 + (0.18^2)/2) \cdot 1}{0.18\sqrt{1}} \approx -0.1097665.

    Then, since d2=d1−σTd_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}, we have that d2=−0.1097665−0.181≈−0.29d_2 = -0.1097665 - 0.18\sqrt{1} \approx -0.29.


  6. Question

    The hedge ratio of an at-the-money call option on Stock PLO is 0.48. The hedge ratio of an at-the-money put option on Stock PLO is -0.55.

    What is the hedge ratio of an at-the-money (long) straddle position on Stock PLO? Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, -0.61.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: −0.07-0.07.

    The hedge ratio of a (long) straddle is the hedge ratio of the call plus the hedge ratio of the put.

    The hedge ratio of the call is 0.48 and the hedge ratio of the put is -0.55, then the hedge ratio of the long straddle is 0.48 + -0.55 = -0.07.


  7. Question

    What would be the dollar change in the value of the portfolio in response to a $6\$6 decline in the stock price? Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, $90.451 becomes 90.45


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 370.8370.8.

    We have 21 shares of stock, 115 put options and a hedge ratio of -0.72.

    The shares of stock collectively change by 21⋅−$6=$−12621 \cdot -\$6 = \$-126.

    The put increases by 115⋅−−0.72⋅−$6=$496.8115 \cdot --0.72 \cdot -\$6 = \$496.8.

    Overall, the portfolio value changes by $−126+$496.8=$370.8\$-126 + \$496.8 = \$370.8.


  8. Question

    Consider the Black-Scholes world and suppose that the delta of a one-year call option on Stock XYZ with a strike price of $350 is 0.65.

    What is the delta of a one-year put option on Stock XYZ with a strike price of $350? Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, -0.21.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: −0.35-0.35.

    The call option delta is N(d1)N(d_1), and the put option delta is N(d1)−1N(d_1) - 1.

    The delta of the call option is N(d1)=0.65N(d_1) = 0.65, then the delta of the put option is given by 0.65−1=−0.350.65 - 1 = -0.35.


  9. Question

    Suppose you own 10 shares of Microsoft stock as well as 26 six-month put options on Microsoft stock. The put options have a hedge ratio of -0.33.

    What is the effective number of shares in your portfolio? Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, 2.64.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 1.421.42.

    The effective number of shares in your portfolio is the actual number of shares minus the effective number of shares from owning the put options.

    You own 10 shares of stock and 26 put options. The hedge ratio is 0.33. The effective number of shares in your portfolio is then: 10−0.33⋅26≈1.4210 - 0.33 \cdot 26 \approx 1.42.
