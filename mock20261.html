  4. Question

    Evaluate the following two statements:

    1. All else equal, a call option with a higher strike price has a lower delta (hedge ratio) than one with a lower strike price.
    2. All else equal, an at-the-money call option has a lower delta (hedge ratio) than a call that is in the money.

    1. Only Statement (ii) is correct
    2. Both statements are correct
    3. Only Statement (i) is correct
    4. None of the statements is correct

    Solution

    The hedge ratio of a call option is N(d1). Note that d1 (and therefore N(d1)) is decreasing in X and increasing in S0/X. Therefore, a call that is in-the-money (S0 > X) has a higher d1 than a call that is at-the-money (S0 = X). Moreover, a call that has a higher strike price X has a lower d1.

  7. Question

    When adding real estate to an asset allocation program that currently includes only stocks, bonds, and cash, which of the properties of real estate returns affects portfolio risk?

    1. Standard deviation.
    2. Covariance with returns of other asset classes.

    1. Only property (ii)
    2. Only property (i)
    3. Both properties
    4. None of the properties

    Solution

    After real estate is added to the portfolio, there are four asset classes in the portfolio: stocks, bonds, cash, and real estate. Portfolio variance now includes a variance term for real estate returns and a covariance term for real estate returns with returns for each of the other three asset classes. Therefore, portfolio risk is affected by the variance (or standard deviation) of real estate returns and the correlation between real estate returns and returns for each of the other asset classes. (Note that the correlation between real estate returns and returns for cash is most likely zero.)


    1. False
    2. False
    3. True
    4. False

    JNJ’s stock price is $90 per share, and the annualized risk-free rate is 4%.

    You believe that the true volatility of the stock is 32% and the respective call delta is 0.5945. Given this data, you construct a delta-neutral portfolio comprising positions in calls and puts that will profit when the option prices come back into alignment. 

    How many put options do you purchase for one call option?

    Note: Enter your answer as decimals (not percentages), rounded to two (2) digits to the right of the decimal point. Use periods for decimal points, not commas. Example: If your solution is 53.367, enter your answer as 53.37. (Enter negative numbers with a leading minus sign.)


    Solution

    Using the “true” volatility of 0.32, and the call delta of 0.5945, the put delta is 0.5945 − 1.0 = −0.4055.

    Therefore, for each call purchased, buy 0.5945/0.4055 = 1.466 = 1.47 puts

    Solution

    Call: $60 + ($5.92) + $3.47 = $69.39 (break-even);
    Put: −$3.47 + $60 + (−$5.92) = $50.61 (break-even);

    Consider a 3-month call option on Stock ABC with a strike price of $80. Stock ABC is currently worth $80 and the call premium is $5.92. The hedge ratio of the call option is 0.54.

    Calculate the elasticity of the call option.

    Note: Enter your answer as decimals (no percentages), rounded to two (2) digits to the right of the decimal point. Use periods for decimal points, not commas. Example: If your solution is 53.367, enter your answer as 53.37. (Enter negative numbers with a leading minus sign.)


    Solution

    We work with a $1 increase in the stock price of Stock ABC to quantify elasticity. So, a change of one dollar in the stock price is 1/80 = 0.0125 = 1.25%.

    The call increases by $1⋅𝐻𝑅 = $1⋅0.54 = $0.54. This represents a relative change of $0.54 / $5.92 = 0.09122 = 9.12%.

    The elasticity of the call option is then 9.12 / 1.25 = 7.296 = 7.30
