  1. Question

    Consider a risky asset that has an expected return of 12.1% and a standard deviation of 19.8%.
    The risk-free rate is 4%.

    Suppose Elena is a utility-maximizing investor with utility function:

    U=𝔼(rp)−12Aσp2U = \mathbb{E}(r_p) - \frac{1}{2} A \sigma^2_p

    where
    - 𝔼(rp)\mathbb{E}(r_p) is the expected return of the portfolio,
    - σp2\sigma^2_p is the variance of the portfolio,
    - AA is Elena’s risk aversion coefficient.

    Elena creates a portfolio by investing a proportion ww in the risky asset and (1−w)(1 - w) in the risk-free asset.

    If she finds it optimal to invest w*w^{*} = 52% in the risky asset, what is her level of risk aversion AA?

    Round your answer to one decimal place.


    Solution

    The optimal weight in the risky asset is:

    w*=μ−rfAσ2⇒A=μ−rfw*⋅σ2w^* = \frac{\mu - r_f}{A \sigma^2} \Rightarrow A = \frac{\mu - r_f}{w^* \cdot \sigma^2}

    (See Lecture 1 for a derivation).

    With:
    - μ=0.121\mu = 0.121,
    - rf=0.04r_f = 0.04,
    - w*=0.52w^* = 0.52,
    - σ2=(0.198)2=0.0392\sigma^2 = (0.198)^2 = 0.0392

    Plugging in, we get:

    A=0.0810.52⋅0.0392=4A = \frac{0.081}{0.52 \cdot 0.0392} = 4.


  2. Question

    Consider a dividend-paying stock DEF. The stock currently trades at $120 per share.

    After two weeks, the stock pays a dividend of 3.

    After a month, three possible market states could occur, with the following probabilities and outcomes:

    State Probability Ending Price Dividend
    Growth 45% $130 $3
    Neutral 20% $120 $3
    Decline 35% $114 $3

    What is the expected holding period return (HPR) of stock DEF?

    Give your answer rounded to three decimal places (e.g., 0.064 for 6.4%).


    Solution

    We compute the HPR for each state:

    • Growth: HPRG=130+3−120120=0.108HPR_G = \frac{130 + 3 - 120}{120} = 0.108

    • Neutral: HPRN=120+3−120120=3120=0.025HPR_N = \frac{120 + 3 - 120}{120} = \frac{3}{120} = 0.025

    • Decline: HPRD=114+3−120120=−0.025HPR_D = \frac{114 + 3 - 120}{120} = -0.025

    Then: HPR = 0.45⋅0.108+0.2⋅0.025+0.35⋅−0.025=0.0450.45 \cdot 0.108 + 0.2 \cdot 0.025 + 0.35 \cdot -0.025 = 0.045

  5. Question

    Consider two perfectly negatively correlated risky securities, A and B.

    • Security A has an expected return of 10.9% and a standard deviation of 24%.
    • Security B has an expected return of 11.2% and a standard deviation of 17.3%.

    If you wish to obtain the global minimum variance portfolio, how much weight wAw_A should you allocate to security A?

    Give your answer as a decimal number, rounded to two decimal places. For example: 0.58.


    Solution

    Note that the minimum variance you can obtain is zero, as the two assets are perfectly negatively correlated.

    As in the tutorial, we then solve for (wAσA−wBσB)2=0(w_A \sigma_A - w_B \sigma_B)^2 = 0 or (wAσA−(1−wa)σB)2=0(w_A \sigma_A - (1-w_a)\sigma_B)^2 = 0.

    The solution is equal to: wA=σBσA+σB=17.324+17.3=0.42w_A = \frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_A + \sigma_B} = \frac{17.3}{24 + 17.3} = 0.42.

  7. Question

    Suppose you have 100 observations of daily returns on a $70000 portfolio. The five biggest losses (negative returns) are: 7.2%, 6.7%, 5.5%, 4.6%, 4.4% (so the fifth biggest loss was a return of -4.4%).

    Given this data, what is the historical VaR(1 day, 95%)?

    Give your answer in monetary terms. Round your answer to the nearest integer, and remember to skip the minus sign. Example: If your calculated VaR is -$5322.43, enter your solution as 5322.


    Solution

    The Value-at-Risk (VaR) is the cut-off at which the tail of the worst outcomes starts, so it is the best return out of the worst 5% of returns. It is the smallest loss (in percentages) multiplied by the dollar value of the portfolio.

    The smallest loss is 4.4% and the portfolio value is $70000, so the VaR is 4.4% x $70000 = $3080.


  8. Question

    Security X has an expected rate of return of 11% and a standard deviation of 15%.
    Security Z has an expected rate of return of 7% and a standard deviation of 8%.
    The securities have a correlation coefficient of 0.59.

    You construct a portfolio by investing 68% in X and the rest in Z.

    What is the variance of the portfolio?
    Round your answer to four decimal places. For example, 0.02579 (or 2.579%) becomes 0.0258.


    Solution

    We use the formula:
    Var(Rp)=wX2σX2+wZ2σZ2+2wXwZσXσZρX,Z\text{Var}(R_p) = w_X^2 \sigma_X^2 + w_Z^2 \sigma_Z^2 + 2 w_X w_Z \sigma_X \sigma_Z \rho_{X,Z}

    With wX=68/100w_X = 68/100, wZ=1−68/100w_Z = 1-68/100, and the standard deviations in decimals, we compute:
    Var(Rp)=(68/100)2⋅(15/100)2+(1−68/100)2⋅(8/100)2+2⋅(68/100)⋅(1−68/100)⋅(15/100)⋅(8/100)⋅0.59=0.0141\text{Var}(R_p) = (68/100)^2 \cdot (15/100)^2 + (1-68/100)^2 \cdot (8/100)^2 + 2 \cdot (68/100) \cdot (1-68/100) \cdot (15/100) \cdot (8/100) \cdot 0.59 = 0.0141

    Note that the standard deviation needs to be a fraction in this equation, so a standard deviation of 14%, for example, becomes 0.14.


  9. Question

    When comparing portfolios A and B, note their respective Sharpe ratios (SR).

    • Portfolio A has a SR of 0.19, based on monthly observations.
    • Portfolio B has a SR of 0.35, based on quarterly observations.

    Calculate and report the SR, on a quarterly basis, for the portfolio with the lower performance.

    Enter your answer as decimals, rounded to two digits to the right of the decimal point. For example, 1.234 becomes 1.23.


    Solution

    To compare the SFR of portfolio A (monthly) and portfolio B (quarterly), we need to first adjust the monthly SR of portfolio A to quarters (3 months).

    SRAq=SRAm×3=0.19×√3=0.33\text{SR}_A^{\text{q}} = \text{SR}_A^{\text{m}} \times \sqrt{3} = 0.19 × √3 = 0.33.

    Now compare:

    • SRAq=0.33SR_A^{q} = 0.33
    • SRBq=0.35SR_B^{q} = 0.35

    Thus, the lower quarterly Sharpe ratio is: 0.33\boxed{0.33}.


  10. Question

    Suppose you are a fund manager who manages the risky portfolio ABC. Portfolio ABC has an expected return of 14% and a standard deviation of 28%.
    The risk-free rate is 2%.

    Your client decides to split their money between your risky portfolio ABC and T-bills, in proportion ww and (1−w)(1 - w), respectively. They want their overall portfolio to have a standard deviation of at most 16%.

    What is the expected total return on your client’s overall portfolio?

    State your answer as a percentage and round to two decimal places. For example, 16.879% becomes 16.88.


    Solution

    To find the desired weight ww to invest in the risky portfolio ABC, we need to first realize that the T-bills have a standard deviation of 0%.

    The desired weight in risky portfolio ABC is then w=σtarget/σABC=16%28%w = \sigma_{\text{target}}/\sigma_{ABC} = \frac{16\%}{28\%}.

    The expected total return of such a portfolio is: Expected Return=(16%28%)⋅14%+(1−16%28%)⋅2%=8.86%\text{Expected Return} = \left( \frac{16\%}{28\%} \right) \cdot 14\% + \left(1 - \frac{16\%}{28\%} \right) \cdot 2\% = 8.86\%.
