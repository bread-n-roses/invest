  1. Question

    Consider Stock ABC which has a current stock price of $37\$37.

    We construct a two-period binomial tree, where the first period is in 6 months from now, and the second period is in one year from now. Every half year, we have that the stock can either go up with u=1.02u = 1.02 or down with d=0.98d = 0.98.

    After two periods (one year), you have constructed three possible stock prices. Report the middle one of the three stock prices, i.e. not the minimum and not the maximum. Round your answer to the nearest integer. For example, 36.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 3737.

    After six months (one period), you have that S1u=S0⋅u=$37.74S_1^u = S_0 \cdot u = \$37.74 and S1d=S0⋅d=$36.985S_1^d = S_0 \cdot d = \$36.985.

    After one year (two periods), you have that S1u2=S0⋅u2=$38.495S_1^{u^2} = S_0 \cdot u^2 = \$38.495 and S1d2=S0⋅d2=$35.5348S_1^{d^2} = S_0 \cdot d^2 = \$35.5348 and S1ud=S0⋅u⋅d≈S0=$37S^{ud}_1 = S_0 \cdot u \cdot d \approx S_0 = \$37.

    The middle stock price is $37\$37.


  2. Question

    Consider a 1-year option with strike price $46\$46, on a stock with annual standard deviation 0.250.25. The T-bill rate is 3% per year.

    What is N(d1)N(d_1) in the Black-Scholes world when the current stock price is $48\$48?

    Round your answer to four decimal places. For example, 0.5238.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 0.66280.6628.

    Recall that d1=ln(S0/X)+(r+σ2/2)TσTd_1 = \frac{ln(S_0/X) + (r + \sigma^2/2) T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.

    Filling in what we know, we obtain d1=ln(48/46)+(0.03+(0.252)/2)⋅10.251≈0.42d_1 = \frac{ln(48/46) + (0.03 + (0.25^2)/2) \cdot 1}{0.25\sqrt{1}} \approx 0.42.

    Then, we have that N(d1)=N(0.42)≈0.6628N(d_1) = N(0.42) \approx 0.6628.


  3. Question

    Option Strike Price Delta
    A 90 12.00 0.65
    B 100 6.00 0.25

    You believe call option B is overpriced relative to call option A. You decide to exploit this using a delta-neutral strategy, where you go long in call option A and short in call option B.

    If you buy 600 options of call A, how many options of call B do you need to short to make the portfolio delta-neutral?

    Round your answer to the nearest whole number. For example, 280.


    Solution

  4. Question

    Suppose that JPMorgan Chase sells call options on $2.8\$2.8 million worth of a stock portfolio with β=1.8\beta = 1.8. The call option delta is 0.62. JPMorgan Chase wishes to hedge its resultant exposure to a market increase by buying a market-index portfolio.

    How many dollars’ worth of the market-index portfolio should it purchase? For example, 4,126,500 becomes 4126500.

    Hint: start by assuming that the market index increases by 1%1\%.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 31248003124800.

    First, to calculate the hedge ratio, suppose that the market index increases by 1%1\%.

    The stock portfolio beta is 1.8 and the stock portfolio is worth 2.8 million. The stock portfolio would then be expected to increase by: 1% ⋅\cdot 1.8 = 1.8% or 0.018 ⋅\cdot 2800000 = 50400.

    The call option delta is 0.62. Since JPMorgan is short the options, its liability increases by 50400 ⋅\cdot 0.62 = 31248.

    In other words, JP Morgan Chase’s liability from writing (selling) these call options would increase by 31248.

    Therefore, JP Morgan wants to hedge its position so that a 1% change in the market index would result in a 31248 change in the value of the market index portfolio. If 1% is 31248, then 100% must be 31248 ⋅100\cdot 100 = 3124800.

    Hence, JP Morgan should purchase 3124800 worth of the market index portfolio.


  5. Question

    A one-year call option with X=$134X = \$134 on a stock priced at S0=$130S_0 = \$130 sells for $10.31\$10.31 in the market.

    Based on historical data, you estimate a volatility of σ=0.4\sigma = 0.4. You find that N(d1)=0.5494N(d_1) = 0.5494 and N(d2)=0.3914N(d_2) = 0.3914. The risk-free interest rate is 0%0\%.

    What is the difference between the Black-Scholes call price and the price of the call in the market? In other words, by how much is the call underpriced in the market?

    Round your answer to two decimals. For example, $12.839 becomes 12.84.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 8.668.66.

    According to the Black-Scholes model, the call should be priced at C0=S0N(d1)−Xe−rf⋅TN(d2)C_0 = S_0 N(d_1) - X e^{-r_f \cdot T}N(d_2).

    The current stock price is $130\$130, the strike price is $134\$134, and the call option premium in the market is $10.31\$10.31. Since the risk-free rate is 0, e−rf⋅Te^{-r_f \cdot T} becomes 1.

    According to Black-Scholes, the call should be worth: C0=$130⋅0.5494−$134⋅1⋅0.3914≈$18.97C_0 = \$130 \cdot 0.5494 - \$134 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.3914 \approx \$18.97.

    The difference is $18.97−$10.31=$8.66\$18.97 - \$10.31 = \$8.66. The call option is underpriced by $8.66\$8.66.


  6. Question

    Consider a 3-month call option on Stock ABC with a strike price of $89\$89. Stock ABC is currently worth $89\$89 and the call premium is $4.84\$4.84. The hedge ratio of the call option is 0.74.

    Calculate the elasticity of the call option. Round your answer to two decimals. For example, 8.31.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 13.6113.61.

    We work with a $1\$1 increase in the stock price of Stock ABC to quantify elasticity.

    The stock increases from $89\$89 to $90\$90, which is an increase of $90−$89$89=0.0112=1.12%\frac{\$90-\$89}{\$89} = 0.0112 = 1.12\%.

    The call HR is 0.74 and the call premium is $4.84\$4.84. The call increases by $1⋅HR=$1⋅0.74=$0.74\$1 \cdot HR = \$1 \cdot 0.74 = \$0.74. This represents a relative change of $0.74/$4.84≈0.1529≈15.29%\$0.74/\$4.84 \approx 0.1529 \approx 15.29\%.

    The elasticity of the call option is then 0.1529/0.0112≈13.610.1529/0.0112 \approx 13.61.


  7. Question

    Suppose you own 17 shares of Microsoft stock as well as 30 six-month put options on Microsoft stock. The put options have a hedge ratio of -0.28.

    What is the effective number of shares in your portfolio? Round your answer to two decimal places. For example, 2.64.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 8.68.6.

    The effective number of shares in your portfolio is the actual number of shares minus the effective number of shares from owning the put options.

    You own 17 shares of stock and 30 put options. The hedge ratio is 0.28. The effective number of shares in your portfolio is then: 17−0.28⋅30≈8.617 - 0.28 \cdot 30 \approx 8.6.


  8. Question

    You buy one Loews June 60 call and one June 60 put. The call premium is $4.93\$4.93 and the put premium is $3.55\$3.55.

    At expiration, there are two potential stock prices at which you would break even. Report the lowest of the two stock prices at which you would break even.

    Round your answer to two decimal places, for example: $54.32.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 51.5251.52.

    Break-even = Strike − total premiums.

    Call: $60+$4.93+$3.55=$68.48\$60 + \$4.93 + \$3.55 = \$68.48 (break-even)

    Put: −$3.55+$60+−$4.93=$51.52−\$3.55 + \$60 + −\$4.93 = \$51.52 (break-even).

    Thus, if the price increases above $68.48\$68.48 or decreases below $51.52\$51.52, a profit is realized. The lowest stock price at which you break even is 51.52.

    Solution

    The current stock price is $28\$28, the risk-free rate is 0.05 and the strike price is $42\$42, so the put option price is: 42⋅exp⁡(−0.05⋅0.25)⋅(1−0.0035)−28⋅(1−0.0055)≈$13.4870937≈$13.4942 \cdot \exp(-0.05 \cdot 0.25) \cdot (1-0.0035) - 28 \cdot (1-0.0055) \approx \$13.4870937 \approx \$13.49.


  10. Question

    A call option trading on a non-dividend paying stock has a market price of $6.9\$6.9. However, you estimate the annualized volatility to be 0.260.26 and use the Black-Scholes model to calculate the option’s theoretical price, which comes out to $5.2\$5.2.

    Assuming that a 5 percentage point increase in volatility raises the Black-Scholes price by approximately $0.50\$0.50, estimate the market-implied volatility that would justify the observed option price of $6.9\$6.9.

    Round your answer to two decimals. For example, 0.285 becomes 0.29.


    Solution

    The correct answer is: 0.430.43.

    We compute the difference between the market price and the theoretical price: $6.9−$5.2=$1.7\$6.9 - \$5.2 = \$1.7.

    We are told that each 5% increase in volatility raises the option price by approximately $0.50\$0.50, hence we have that 1.70.50=3.4\frac{1.7}{0.50} = 3.4.

    Hence, the implied volatility must be higher with 3.4⋅0.05=0.173.4 \cdot 0.05 = 0.17 or 17 percentage points.

    The implied volatility that explains the observed market price is approximately 0.17+0.26=0.430.17 + 0.26 = 0.43.
